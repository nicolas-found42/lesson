Unit 4 · Lesson 4.6

Conflicting requirements: spot them, surface them, choose

Two things you ask for can pull in opposite directions. Make the conflict visible before the agent builds around an assumption you never approved.

About 15 minutesBuilds on: 4.1 Definition of doneWin: you can make an agent list its conflicts and choose one yourself, in writing

Why this matters for shipping your app

Shipping your app means deciding what matters most when you cannot have everything. “Make it fast and show fifty large photos” may hide a choice about speed versus detail. If the agent drops one requirement without asking, you could receive a working page that misses your purpose.

Learn three moves. Spot a possible conflict. Ask the agent to explain it. Then choose on purpose, before it builds.

Two "musts" can contradict

Conflicting requirements cannot all be met under the same conditions. “Show the latest scores while offline” conflicts with “get every score from the internet right now.” Other pairs are tensions, not proven contradictions. Fifty large photos and a fast page might both work, depending on image sizes and the connection. Ask for evidence before you call a target impossible.

Catch a conflict early and it costs you one question. Catch it late and it costs you a rebuild, because the agent has already shaped the app around one side of the pair.

The agent might choose without asking

Anthropic's documentation on instruction files warns that if two instructions contradict each other, Claude may pick one arbitrarily.[1] That warning concerns Claude Code's rules files. It is not a general rule about every agent. Still, it is a good reason to ask for conflicts out loud. Silence is not agreement.

Separate three cases. An unclear phrase needs a definition. A difficult target needs looking into. Requirements that cannot both hold need your decision. “This seems hard” is not proof that you must give something up. Ask which requirements clash, under what conditions, and what evidence backs the claim.

A real pattern, anonymized

A user wanted a simulation to run roughly a thousand times faster while still showing every stage smoothly. The agent measured the current app, tried changes, and reported that the combination was not achievable with that implementation and computer. It undid failed experiments and asked which requirement to relax. The useful result was not a promised speedup: it was a visible decision between watching all the detail and completing more work quickly. More research could explore alternatives, but it did not itself settle the user's priority.

Make the agent surface conflicts before it builds

Ask the agent to list conflicts before it edits. Anthropic's best-practices page recommends interviewing the user: It keeps asking until the answers add up to a spec.[2] A spec is a written description of what to build. Claude Code's question tool likewise exists to gather requirements or clarify ambiguity.[3] You do not need a special tool here. The same plain prompt works in Claude Code, Codex and omp.

Copy this — works in any agent 
Do not edit files yet. Check my requirements for conflicts.
For each possible conflict, name the two requirements, explain why they
clash, and separate evidence from assumptions. Suggest two choices and
recommend one based on my goal. Ask which choice I want, then wait.
After I answer, restate the revised acceptance criteria before editing.

Three phrases do the work. “Do not edit” keeps the checking apart from the building. “Evidence from assumptions” stops a guess from becoming a reason to cut your feature. “Wait” keeps the product decision with you. These are requests, not guarantees. Read the reply and check that it names both sides.

Write the answer as one line, in the form “X vs Y — because Z”. For the gallery: “instant loading vs fifty full-size photos — because each photo adds load time.” That line is easy to check, and easy to paste into your plan later.

Same request, with and without the instruction

Without it — silent pick

"Make the gallery page load instantly and put all fifty full-size photos on it." The agent ships a page with ten compressed thumbnails. Speed was easier to satisfy. It never mentions that the two asks fought.

With it — conflict surfaced

Same request, plus the prompt block above. The agent replies: "Instant loading vs fifty full-size photos — each photo adds load time. Which wins?" You answer "loading speed". The plan says so in one sentence.

When a conflict reaches you: choose deliberately

A trade-off gains something by giving something else up. Choose from your app's audience and purpose, not from whichever option sounds most technical. A compromise can be right: fewer photos at first, more on request. Approve it on purpose anyway. Ask what the user gains, what they lose, and how the new result will be checked.

Priority wordMeaningExample
MustNon-negotiable; the app fails without it"Loads in under two seconds"
ShouldStrongly wanted; can be traded away in a conflict"Shows photo captions on hover"
NiceDelight only; first thing to drop when something must give"Animated transitions between photos"

These are priority labels for your project, not tool settings. They help explain what can change. They cannot stop two “musts” from clashing. Do not quietly demote either one. Tell the agent: “If meeting one must would break another, show me the conflict and wait.” A recommendation is useful. Permission to change your priorities is a separate thing.

A demotion you did not choose is still a demotion. If the agent decides your captions are “nice” rather than “should”, the next version may drop them without a word. The labels only help if you set them.

Write the choice down

Reply with an explicit replacement: “Loading speed wins. Show ten photos initially and let visitors request the rest. Keep the two-second target, measured under the conditions we agreed.” Then ask the agent to rewrite the acceptance criteria from 4.1. The old demand should go, not sit beside the new one. Check the result against that revised list. Do not accept the agent's word that it found a compromise.

Write the decision where you will see it again: in the spec, not only in the chat. A conflict you settled last week is a requirement you do not have to argue about twice.

Recall before you read on

Without scrolling up: when two of your requirements conflict, what are the three moves this lesson teaches, and who makes the final choice?

Spot a possible incompatibility; surface both requirements and the evidence; choose which requirement changes. You make the product decision, then ask the agent to rewrite the acceptance criteria.

Do it now: surface one real conflict

You should see the conflicting pair, a reason, options, and a pause for your decision. After your answer, the new criteria should reflect the choice without retaining the incompatible old demand. If the agent reports no conflict, compare its interpretation with yours; do not invent a conflict merely to complete the exercise.

Check yourself

Question 1 of 3

You ask for a page that loads instantly and also shows fifty big photos. The agent ships a fast page with ten small thumbnails and says nothing. What happened?

The visible result no longer matches your photo requirement. Whatever the agent's reasoning, it changed the requirement without your decision. Ask it to surface the conflict and revise the criteria with you.

Question 2 of 3

Which line, added to a prompt, best makes an agent surface conflicts before building?

Listing pairs first, then asking and waiting, forces the conflict onto paper and the decision to you. The other lines either delay the conflict past the build or hand the choice back to the agent.

Question 3 of 3

The agent reports: "fast loading vs fifty photos — they conflict". What is the best next move?

Once the conflict has evidence, choose deliberately and update what counts as done. Another attempt or an unapproved compromise does not resolve the requirements.
