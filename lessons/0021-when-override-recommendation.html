When to override a recommendation

Every question in an interview comes with an answer the agent would pick for you. Some of those answers are technical guesses you should accept. A few are decisions about your product, and those are yours.

About 15 minutesBuilds on: 1.5 "Yes to all"Win: you can spot a product decision and overrule it with a reason

Why this matters for shipping your app

You are building something other people will open. The agent will never meet them. It knows the files, the request you typed, and a great deal about how software is usually built. What it does not know is what your app should feel like to the person on the other end.

If you accept every recommended answer, you still ship something. It is just shaped by the agent's assumptions instead of your intent. The difference rarely shows up as a bug. It shows up as an app that opens on the wrong screen, buries the one action you cared about, or feels like a tool built for someone else.

What "recommended" actually means

In the interview pattern from lesson 1.4, each question arrives with a default: the option the agent would choose if you said "whatever you think". Anthropic's guidance for asking an agent to interview you describes the shape of that exchange: Claude comes back with multiple-choice questions about the parts you haven’t pinned down.[1] It keeps going until the answers form a written spec: a short file describing what should exist when the work is done.[1]

That default is a proposal, built from three things: general norms about software, the words of your request, and whatever it could read in your folder. None of those is knowledge of your users. OpenAI's Codex guide goes further in the same direction, suggesting you ask Codex to question you first and to challenge your assumptions and turn the fuzzy idea into something concrete.[2] The recommendation is the agent's best guess, offered so you can move quickly. It is not a verdict.

Two kinds of question, one test

Interview questions fall into two rough groups. Product intent questions decide what the app is like to use: what appears first, what a beginner sees, what gets hidden, what the tone is. Mechanism questions decide how the thing is built: file layout, which library, how a page is generated. The agent is usually better than you at mechanism. You are the only source of intent.

QuestionKindWho decides
"Should the whole app be one page or several?"MechanismAccept the recommendation
"What should a first-time visitor see when it opens?"Product intentYou
"Where should the stylesheet live?"MechanismAccept the recommendation
"Should search be the first action, or should everything be visible first?"Product intentYou

Two questions separate them in practice.

  1. Would a user notice or feel this? Not "would they see the file", but would the experience change. A folder name is invisible. A default screen is the whole first impression.
  2. Could I want the opposite, for a reason I can say in one sentence? "My relatives will open this once and never read instructions" is a reason. "It feels wrong" is a feeling worth naming too, but turning it into a sentence is what lets the agent apply it elsewhere.

If both answers are yes, the decision is yours and the recommendation is only a starting point. If either is no, accept it and save your attention. A third question helps with the borderline cases: how expensive is this to change later? A cheap, invisible choice can stay with the agent even if you have an opinion.

A useful asymmetry

The agent can be wrong about your product and still be right about your code. Overriding it on layout does not mean doubting it on how the layout is built.

Override with a reason, not a verdict

Comparing two ways to reject the same recommendation:

Override as a verdict

"Q4: no. Full network."

The agent knows your choice and nothing else. Every later question that depends on it is a fresh guess, and the reason may never reach the code.

Override with the reason

"Q4: full network first. Someone opening this cold needs to see the shape before searching for one person. Keep search visible, but not as the opening screen."

The agent now knows the audience ("someone opening this cold"), the intent (see the shape), and the constraint (search stays visible). It can apply all three to questions you never answered.

This is not politeness. Anthropic's prompting guide notes that Providing context or motivation behind your instructions gives the model more information about your goals and produces more targeted results.[3] A one-line reason is the cheapest form of that context, and it survives into the next turn.

It also composes with the habit from lesson 1.5. "Yes to all, except the opening screen: show everything first" is one message that accepts twenty defaults and corrects the one that matters.

A real pattern, anonymized

A user asked an agent to make an app that maps connections between records feel less clunky for beginners. The agent interviewed them for about half an hour: twenty-six questions, each with a recommended answer. The user replied "yes to all" several times, and overrode exactly three answers. All three were about what a newcomer sees first: open on the whole picture rather than a search box, make finding one specific record the obvious next action, and design for a desktop screen before a phone. The agent's recommendations had pointed the other way on each, toward search-first and both-screens-at-once.

The user never argued about how the app was built. The three overrides changed the default view, the hint text on the map, and the order of the controls. Those were the parts a first-time visitor would actually meet.

When not to override

Override too often and the interview stops being an interview. If you disagree with nearly every recommendation, the likely cause is upstream: the request described a feeling ("make it less clunky") without saying who it was clunky for. Fix the goal (lesson 1.1), rerun the questions (lesson 1.4), and the defaults get closer. The three overrides above were right precisely because they were rare and specific.

Two more cases where "override" is the wrong move:

  • Safety defaults are not taste. A recommendation to keep approval prompts on, or to avoid putting a key in the code, is not a product opinion. Keep prompts on; lesson 0.6 explains the modes, and Unit 8 covers secrets.
  • Factual claims need evidence, not a vote. If the agent says a host has a size limit, overriding it does not change the limit. Ask for the source, then decide what to do about it.
Agents do not always surface the trade-offs inside their own recommendations. Claude Code ships an optional notice that looks for a tradeoff Claude chose without calling it out; it is off by default.[4] Asking "what did you decide for me here?" is the manual version, and it works in every tool.

Where the questions show up

All three agents can run an interview, but the mechanics differ. What does not differ: you answer in your own words whenever the offered options do not fit.

Claude Code has a built-in question tool named AskUserQuestion, listed in its permissions documentation among tools that require user interaction.[5] Its best-practices page describes the interview as several rounds of multiple-choice questions written to a spec file.[1] If no option says what you mean, type the answer instead of picking the nearest one.

Codex's guide recommends plan mode for tasks that are complex, ambiguous, or hard to describe well, and suggests asking Codex to question you first and to challenge your assumptions.[2] Treat each proposed answer as an opening position, then say which one you are changing and why.

omp's usage guide frames this as one of the ways you intervene: Send a steering message while it works, answer a question, or reject an unsafe action.[6] Its documentation comes only from its own authors, so confirm the exact prompt shape in your session.

Recall before you read on

Without scrolling up: give the two-question test that tells a product decision from a technical one, and say what an override should always include.

Ask whether a user would notice or feel the choice, and whether you could want the opposite for a reason you can state in one sentence. If both are yes, it is product intent and yours to decide. An override should carry that reason, not just the verdict, so the agent can apply it to later questions.

Practice: sort the agent's defaults

This works on any project, before any code is written. You are not building yet; you are finding out which decisions were about to be made without you.

Do this now 
Before you change any files, list the five decisions you would otherwise make for me in this task.
For each one: the options, the option you recommend, and one sentence on what that recommendation is based on.
Mark each as "product" (what a user sees or feels) or "technical" (how it is built).
Do not edit anything yet.

A numbered list of five rows, each with a recommendation and its basis. The product rows are the ones where the basis sounds like a guess about people ("most users prefer…", "a typical layout…") rather than a fact about your folder. Those are the rows to check first. If every row is marked technical, your request may be too narrow to have product decisions in it, which is worth knowing too.

Check yourself

Question 1 of 3

The agent recommends one option. When is overriding it the right call?

What a first-time user meets is product intent, and the agent cannot know your users. File layout, speed and convention are mechanism: the agent is a better judge, and a wrong call is cheap to change.

Question 2 of 3

What makes an override work well?

The reason is context the agent can reuse on questions you never saw. A bare "no" changes one answer; a reason changes how the rest of the work is aimed.

Question 3 of 3

You disagree with nearly every recommendation. What should you do?

Blanket disagreement usually means the request did not say who the app is for or what it should feel like. Fix the goal and rerun the interview; the defaults get closer and your attention goes where it matters.

Ask your teacher

Unsure whether a recommendation is yours? Paste the question and the recommended answer into a message and ask: "Is this a product decision or a technical one, and what would change if I chose the opposite?" That question is useful even when the answer is "either is fine".
