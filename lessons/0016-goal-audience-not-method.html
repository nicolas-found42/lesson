Goal and audience, not method

Tell the agent what should exist and who it is for. Let it choose the steps. Your two sentences about the person at the other end do more work than twenty about tools.

About 15 minutesBuilds on: 0.7 First prompt to a pageWin: you can write a prompt the agent can aim at

Why this matters for shipping your app

You are going to ship an app to people who are not you. The agent cannot see those people. It can see your folder, and it can read what you type. If you never say who the app is for, the agent fills the gap with a guess, and a guess about strangers is usually "a developer like me". That is how beginners end up with an app that works on their screen and confuses everyone else.

Three things a prompt can contain

A prompt can say what, who or how. The course will use these words the same way every time:

  • Goal: what should be true when the work is done. "My recipe collection is on the web."
  • Audience: who will use the result, and what they cannot or will not do. "My relatives, on their phones, without installing anything."
  • Method: the steps and tools used to get there. "Create a deploy workflow and turn on hosting."

Beginners tend to write only method, because method is what they imagine the work to be. But method is the agent's strong side. It reads your files, measures them, and picks a way. The goal and the audience are the parts only you hold.

The official guides agree on this. Codex's best-practices page recommends a default of four parts, and the first is Goal: What are you trying to change or build? The others are Context, Constraints and "Done when". It says this structure helps Codex stay scoped, make fewer assumptions, and produce work that’s easier to review.[1] Claude Code's page says to name the file, describe the symptom, and say what “fixed” looks like up front.[2] Anthropic's prompting guide offers a picture to hold on to: Think of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows.[3]

The audience usually slots into the "Context" and "Constraints" parts of Codex's four-part prompt. This lesson pulls it out because beginners forget it most often.

The same request, two ways

Method only

"Set up a deploy workflow and turn on free hosting for this folder."

Goal and audience

"I want my recipe collection online so my relatives can open it from a link on their phones. They won't install or sign up for anything. When it works, give me the link and put it at the top of the project's front page."

The first version only works if you already know what "deploy workflow" means and whether it is the right tool. The second leaves the method open and adds three facts the agent could never find alone: the readers use phones, they will not create accounts, and "done" includes a link in a named place. Everything else follows from those facts.

How to say who it is for

"Everyone" is not an audience. It gives the agent nothing to work with. Three short facts do more:

  • The device. "On a phone, held in one hand."
  • The starting knowledge. "They have never used a code repository."
  • The friction they will not accept. "No sign-up, no download, no app store."

Each fact quietly rules out options. A phone in one hand rules out wide tables and menus that open on hover. "No sign-up" rules out anything that asks for a login. "Never used a repository" rules out a deliverable that is a folder to download and open. You do not have to name those options yourself. You only supply the facts, and the agent connects them faster than you can.

Compare "make it easy to use" with "my father will open this on an old phone over a slow connection, so the page must be readable before the images finish loading." The first is a wish. The second lets the agent decide about image size, text size, and where the important part of the page sits. The same goes for "make it quick": ask what a person should be able to do within ten seconds, and the answer becomes something you can check.

The third blank in the template — "when it works, I should be able to…" — is your own check. It must be something you can see for yourself, not something the agent reports. "I can open the link on my phone and read the menu without pinching" is a check. "The deploy is configured" is not, because looking at the page tells you nothing about it.

A real pattern, anonymized

Remember the gallery in lesson 0.1, where one message led to about fifty tool calls? That message was two sentences. One said who the page was for: friends who are not technical and would not know how to use a code repository. The other said where the link should go afterwards. It named no tool and no step. Because the audience was stated, the agent decided that the deliverable was a web address rather than a list of instructions, and it measured the project and changed its plan when it found the folder was too big for free hosting.

Recall before you read on

Without scrolling up: name the three things a prompt can contain, and say which two only you can supply.

A prompt can contain a goal, an audience and a method. Only you hold the goal and the audience. The agent can usually work out the method itself.

A fill-in template

When you are unsure how to start, fill in these blanks. Delete anything that does not apply.

I want [what should exist when we are done].
It is for [who], who [what they cannot or will not do, such as install software or use a laptop].
When it works, I should be able to [one thing I can check myself].
Please do not [one thing I do not want].
If you have to choose between options, pick the simplest one that works and tell me why.

The last line matters more than it looks. It gives the agent permission to decide, and it asks for the reason, so you can learn what it chose.

When method does belong in the prompt

Sometimes you do care about the how. Claude Code's guide says a preference between two reasonable approaches cannot be read from your files: if you care which one it picks, say so up front.[2] "No new libraries", "keep it one file", "no sign-up" are all fair. Write them as constraints on the result, next to the goal. Do not write the whole recipe of steps. A constraint tells the agent what to avoid. A recipe makes you responsible for every step, and you do not yet know the steps.

Do it in your own agent

You should see a page built to your description, opened or described so you can look at it. The agent's answer to the last question should name your audience (phones, no sign-up) and point to specific choices, such as large text or a single column. If it names a generic reader, your audience line was too vague. Rewrite it and try again.

Check yourself

Question 1 of 3

Which prompt gives the agent a goal and an audience?

Only the third says what should exist (photos online) and who it is for (a gran who needs to open them). The others describe steps or tools without a reader.

Question 2 of 3

You wrote only method steps and the agent built something odd. What is the likeliest cause?

Steps tell the agent what to do but not why. Without a reader in mind, it fills the gap with a guess. More steps would only make you responsible for more details.

Question 3 of 3

You really do not want an account system. Where does that belong?

A preference the agent cannot read from your files has to be stated. Write it as a constraint ("no sign-up") next to the goal, not as a recipe.
