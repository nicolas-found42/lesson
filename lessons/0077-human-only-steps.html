Human-only steps: logins, attestations, and runbooks

Some steps of shipping your app are yours alone: typing a password, agreeing to terms, confirming which plan you are on. Your agent cannot do them — and a good agent will write you a numbered list, called a runbook, telling you exactly what to do and when.

Why this matters for shipping

Putting your app on a public URL (the goal of Unit 6) usually touches at least one account: a hosting service, a code host, sometimes a payments page. Those accounts are tied to you. Your agent works inside your project folder and can read files, edit files, and run commands — but it cannot and should not stand in for you when a website asks who you are. Knowing which steps stay human keeps you safe, keeps you legally clean, and stops the agent from guessing its way into your accounts.

Lesson 0.6 taught you to keep approval prompts on, so the agent asks before risky actions. This lesson is about a different boundary: actions no approval setting can hand over, because they need your identity or your consent.

What only a human can do

Three kinds of step belong to you:

Logins. Typing a password, a one-time code, or clicking a "sign in" button in a browser. Every one of the three agents in this course handles its own sign-in this way: Claude Code prompts you to log in on first use[1]; Codex asks you to choose Sign in with ChatGPT or another available sign-in method[2]; omp opens a first-run setup wizard, and OAuth users can reopen sign-in with /login[4]. In each case the tool hands the moment to you rather than handling credentials itself.

Consents. Clicking "I agree" on terms of service, authorizing an app to connect to another service, or approving a permission request on a provider's website. These create legal obligations in your name, so they are yours.

Attestations. Statements only you can truthfully make, such as "this is a free account" or "I own this domain". The agent might read a settings page and guess — but a guess that quietly becomes an assumption in your project is exactly the kind of invented claim lesson 5.8 warns about. An attestation is different from a fact the agent can check: it is a fact about your situation, your ownership, or your intent.

A useful test: could the agent verify this by looking at something? If yes, it is a task. If it needs your identity, your agreement, or your word, it is a human-only step.

What this looks like in a real run

A real pattern, anonymized

A user asked an agent to put a small website on a hosting provider. The agent did everything inside the project — code, checks, the pull request, the merge. Then it stopped at one step: the hosting provider needed a login in a browser window, and a confirmation that the account was on the free plan. The agent could not do either. So it produced four numbered steps for the user: run the provider's login command in the terminal, complete the browser sign-in, confirm the plan choice, then say "done". The user did them in under two minutes, and the agent finished the deploy and verified the live URL. Everything automated was automated; everything human took one focused pause.

Notice the shape: the agent did not fail at the login step. It recognized the step as human-only, packaged it as a short numbered list, and waited. That list is a runbook — a numbered set of instructions for the human, written by the agent, with each step small enough to do without thinking.

Ask for the runbook two ways

Up front. Before a big task, ask the agent to list every human-only step, so you can see the whole shape of the work:

Before you start, list every step of this task that only a human
can do — logins, agreeing to terms, plan or ownership checks, anything
needing my password or my consent. Number them, one sentence each, and
say at which point in the build each one is needed. Do not ask me for
any password or secret key.

As you go. For a longer task, set one rule and let the agent decide when to stop:

Do the whole task. Only stop when you reach a step you absolutely
cannot complete and need me to act. When you stop, give me the exact
numbered steps to do, then wait for me to say when it is done.

The second phrasing — a stop rule rather than a script — works well because the agent discovers human-only steps as it goes, the same way it discovers which commands it needs. You can also write this preference once into your project's rules file, so every session inherits it (lesson 1.12).

Recall before you read on

Without scrolling up: name the three kinds of human-only step, and say what the agent should hand you when it hits one.

Logins (passwords, sign-in buttons, one-time codes), consents (terms, authorizations), and attestations (facts only you can vouch for, like which plan you own). When the agent hits one, it should stop and hand you a numbered runbook, then wait.

Keep secrets out of the handoff

A runbook should tell you what to do, not collect your credentials. Two cautions, both from real runs:

Two habits to keep

Never paste a password or key into the chat — your conversation can end up in logs, and a secret that appears in a screenshot or command output should be treated as leaked and replaced. And when you follow a step that prints anything secret-looking, say so; a good agent will redo the step a safer way. Secrets belong in files your project ignores, or better, entered only where the provider asks (Units 8 and 9 cover this properly).

Approval settings do not protect you here. A permission mode decides which tool calls run without asking — Codex's docs describe the boundary as asks when it needs to go beyond that boundary[3], and omp's docs note its modes describe what runs without a prompt but do not make model output trustworthy[6]. Neither is a guarantee about what appears in your transcript. That judgement stays yours.

Your turn: get your first runbook

Your turn — about 5 minutes

You should see a short numbered list (usually one to five items) with no passwords or keys requested, and after you complete one item and say "done", the agent picks the task back up without you re-explaining anything. If it re-asks questions you already answered, add "continue from the runbook" to your message.

One more thing worth checking in the runbook: does the agent plan to verify its own work afterward? If the list ends at "you log in" with no step for checking the live result, add "and after each of my steps, tell me how you will confirm the task still works" — that habit belongs to Unit 2.

Check yourself

Question 1 of 4

The agent reaches a step that needs your password. What should it do?

Credentials are yours alone. The right move is a short runbook of exact steps and a pause until you confirm you have done them.

Question 2 of 4

What is a runbook, in this lesson's sense?

A runbook is written by the agent for you: small numbered steps for the parts that need your identity or consent, delivered when you reach them.

Question 3 of 4

Which of these is an attestation the agent cannot check by itself?

The first three are things the agent can verify with tools. Your plan, your ownership, and your agreements are facts only you can vouch for.

Question 4 of 4

Why keep passwords and keys out of the chat transcript?

What enters the conversation can persist in session logs, screenshots, or command output. Enter secrets only where the provider asks, and treat an exposed key as leaked.
