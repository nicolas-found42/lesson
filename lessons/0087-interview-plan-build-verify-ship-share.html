Unit 7 · Lesson 7.2 · First-ship path ★

The capstone path: interview, plan, build, verify, ship, share

You already shipped a hello world page. Now you ship the app you actually chose. Six steps, in order, each one already practised on its own — this lesson is about running them as one continuous run.

About 15 minutes of reading, then your capstone runBuilds on: 7.1 Choose an idea and every ★ lesson before itWin: a public link to your app, plus a plan you can reuse for the next one

Why this matters for shipping your app

Your mission is to take an idea to a public app by directing a coding agent. The steps in this lesson are not new machinery. You have met the interview, the plan gate, the run-yourself loop, commits, push, publish, and the live check in earlier lessons. What changes now is that you run them back to back, on one real idea, without stopping to read about each one. The order is the lesson: each step exists because the step before it made something checkable.

The six steps at a glance

StepWhat happensYou have seen it in
1. InterviewThe agent asks numbered questions with a recommended answer; you answer by number.1.4 The interview, 1.5 Yes to all
2. PlanThe agent writes the steps it will take and you give a human "go".1.7 Plan, then a human go
3. BuildThe agent edits files and runs things, with commits along the way.2.1 Run it yourself, 3.13 Checkpoint often
4. VerifyYou open the real thing yourself; "done" stays a claim until you look.2.2 Claims vs evidence, 2.7 Real vs simulated, 2.13 Be a first-time user
5. ShipPush, let the host publish, and confirm the public address answers.6.3 GitHub Pages, 6.6 Deploy, merge, live
6. SharePut the link where your intended users will actually find it.6.7 README and repo description

The steps form a chain of evidence. The interview produces decisions. The plan turns decisions into actions. The build produces files. Verification produces an observation. Shipping produces a public address. Sharing puts that address in front of a person. If a link in the chain is skipped, the later steps inherit the doubt.

A capstone run, end to end

Here is the shape of a real capstone session, rewritten without names or project details. The person had chosen a small idea in lesson 7.1: a site where their hobby club tracks who brings what to each meet.

youInterview me about the potluck tracker until you could write the spec. Ask numbered questions, each with a recommendation. Cap it at eight questions.
agentQ1..Q8 covering who may edit, what an entry needs, and what "done" looks like. Recommendations attached to each.
you1. anyone with the link. 2. your rec. 3. your rec, but show the date. … 8. your rec.
youWrite the plan: files to create, what you will not touch, how you will check. Wait for my go.
agentPlan: create index.html, one CSS file, one JS file; no build step; check = open localhost and click through. Go?
youGo.
toolwrite the three files; start a local server; open localhost; confirm the list renders
toolcommit "first working version"; you look at localhost yourself and confirm
toolpush; enable Pages on the main branch; wait for the build
toolopen the public URL → the page loaded but was blank; missing script path was absolute, host serves from a subpath
toolswitch to relative links; commit; push; wait; open the public URL in a private window → renders correctly
toolwrite the public link at the top of the README; commit; push
agentLive at the URL in the README. I verified it in a private window after the publish finished, not at merge time.
Anonymized from a real capstone session. The blank page was a relative-path bug that only the public address could reveal.

Notice what did not happen. There was no long unsupervised run, no trust in "done", and no publish believed before an address answered. The six steps each did one job.

The one prompt that starts the run

You do not need a special tool or skill for this. One plain message starts the whole chain, because it names the steps as the definition of done:

Build my capstone app: [one sentence describing the idea and who it is for].

Work in this order and stop at each gate:
1. Interview me first: numbered questions, each with a recommended
   answer. Cap it at 8 questions. Wait for my answers.
2. Write the plan (files to create, what you will not touch, how you
   will check it). Wait for my "go".
3. Build it, committing at least once per working feature.
4. Verify: run it, open it yourself in a real browser, and tell me
   what you did NOT verify.
5. Push, publish, and confirm the public URL loads in a fresh
   window before you call it shipped.
6. Put the link at the top of the README so visitors find it.

Keep approval prompts on. Do not disable any safety settings.
Done when: I can open the public link and use the app myself.

The prompt is agent-agnostic by design: it names behaviours, not menu items. OpenAI's prompting guidance frames a good prompt as Goal, Context, Constraints, Done when[2], and this block is exactly those four parts with the gates inserted. If your agent offers a plan mode or a rules file, you may use them, but nothing here depends on them — see 1.12 The rules file for when writing the procedure once starts to pay off.

Build with checkpoints, verify with your own eyes

During the build, ask for a commit after each working feature, not at the end. Pro Git describes a commit as a snapshot you can revert to or compare later[3], and the whole point of the capstone is that your run stays recoverable — lesson 3.13 covers why long unsaved runs are risky. During verification, the rule from lesson 2.2 stands: done is a claim. Open the local page, then the public page, yourself. Claude Code's best-practices page puts the failure plainly: the agent said it was done and it wasn't quite, usually because it had no way to check its own work[4] — so give it a way (run the checks, open the browser) and still look yourself. For the ship step, remember the five states from the milestone: committed, pushed, published, live are four different things, and only the public address is live (M.1, 6.6). GitHub's Pages docs note that changes can take up to 10 minutes to publish after a push[5], so waiting and re-opening the URL is part of the step, not a delay in it.

Share is a step, not an afterthought

Shipping gives you an address. Sharing gives your users the address. Put the link at the top of the README and in the repository's description field, because that is where people look before they click anything (6.7). Then do the first-time-user walk from lesson 2.13: open the live app as if you had never seen it, or hand the link to a friend and read their screenshot. If the first thing a new user does is get confused, that is a finding, and you are still in the loop — fix, commit, push, re-verify the public URL.

Recall before you read on

Without scrolling up: name the six steps in order, and say which step catches a wrong guess most cheaply. Write it in your own words.

Interview → plan → build → verify → ship → share. The interview catches a wrong guess most cheaply, because no files have been changed yet — every later step inherits whatever the earlier steps decided.

Capstone checklist — tick each as you finish it

What you should see: a public link that loads your app in a window that has never seen the project, and a README whose first line is that link. If the public page is wrong while localhost was right, you have found a relative-path or subpath bug — exactly the one in the transcript above.

Check yourself

Question 1 of 4

In the six-step path, where are wrong guesses caught most cheaply?

The interview runs before any file exists, so a wrong assumption costs one line to correct. Every later step inherits the decisions made earlier.

Question 2 of 4

Why does the plan step get a human "go" before building?

The plan is words until you approve it; the "go" is the moment a wrong assumption would otherwise become edits. Lesson 1.7 covers the gate itself.

Question 3 of 4

After the agent says the app is live, what proves it?

Deploy, merge, and live are different states. Only a request to the public address proves the live one — lesson 6.6.

Question 4 of 4

What does "share" add that "ship" does not?

Ship produces an address; share puts it in front of a person. The README top and the repo description are where visitors look first — lesson 6.7.

Keep approvals on

Run the whole capstone with approval prompts enabled. The prompt above says so explicitly, and nothing in this lesson requires a weaker setting. If the agent proposes disabling them, decline — lesson 0.6 explains what the modes mean and why a beginner keeps prompts on.

Ask your teacher

Stuck mid-run? Ask the agent: "What is the task and its state right now?" or "Which of the six steps are we in, and what proves we finished the last one?" Then ask yourself the same question.
