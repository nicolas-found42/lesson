Over-asking: cap the interview before it runs too long

An interview exists to catch wrong guesses before they become code. Past a certain length it stops catching them — and starts costing you the attention it was supposed to save.

Why this matters for shipping your app

Lesson 1.4 gave you the interview: numbered questions, one decision each, a recommended answer you can accept in one word. That technique works because it catches a wrong guess while the fix costs one sentence. But no agent has a built-in sense of enough questions. Anthropic's own example prompt is Interview me until you could write the spec — the stopping point is the spec, and nothing tells the agent that the spec is already writable.[1] Codex's guidance has the same shape: ask Codex to question you first and turn the fuzzy idea into something concrete before writing code.[2] Both stop conditions are about the output, not about how many questions got you there.

So the interview can keep growing. Every extra round costs you reading time, and the classic failure follows: you skim, answer "yes to all", and the unread questions quietly become defaults — the exact thing the interview was invented to prevent. Worse, a question is not free to the run either. Claude Code's documentation for its question tool says Questions stay open until you answer them:[3] each unanswered question is real waiting.

What over-asking looks like

This illustrative rewrite shows the pattern: early questions change the app's direction; later ones repeat low-risk choices. It is not a verbatim session transcript.

agentRound 1 — Q1-3: who uses this, what must never change, desktop or phone first. Recommended: an answer for each.
you1. newcomers. 2. keep browsing first. 3. desktop first.
agentRound 4 — Q8-13: labels, colors, panel order, hint text, density, toggle.
youyes to all
agentRound 7 — Q17-22: motion, drag feel, empty state, errors, zoom, reset.
youUse those defaults, except keep dragging light and movable.
agentRound 9 — Q24-26: confirmation dialogs, then "ready to implement?"
noteThe repeated rounds are the warning sign; not every detail needs a separate approval.

Those later details may matter, but asking about every one separately can exhaust the person making the choices. Bulk acceptance alone does not prove anyone skimmed. The warning is that you notice yourself accepting without reading.

Three signs the interview has run long

  • The question answers itself from the folder. "What does this page do?" — the answer is in the code or the README. Lesson 4.12 covers which questions belong to you and which to the files.
  • You are accepting without reading. Several identical replies can be fine after careful review; they become a warning when you cannot restate what you approved.
  • The answer would change no file. "Should we also support dark mode someday?" is a park-it, not a round.

The test that keeps an interview short

Ask: would the answers materially change this build, or create a risk I need to own? Audience, the first thing a newcomer does, and deletion behavior deserve your attention. A reversible styling detail can usually become a proposed default. If the question is unclear, ask for its consequence rather than guessing. Six questions per round and two rounds total are useful starting caps for a small task, not vendor limits or universal rules.

Put the cap in the prompt

You do not have to hope the agent stops. Say the cap out loud, with the stopping rule, in the same prompt that asks for the interview:

I want [your goal in one sentence, and who it is for].

Before writing any code, interview me: numbered questions, one
decision each, with your recommended answer and a one-line reason.
Hard cap: at most 6 questions per round, at most 2 rounds. Stop
early the moment you could write a one-page spec. For low-risk,
reversible details still open, propose defaults under "Decided by
you" (meaning the agent). List unresolved safety or product-direction
choices as blockers instead of guessing. Show the spec and wait
for my go-ahead. Do not edit files.

The cap limits questions, not care. Finish with your decisions, the agent's proposed defaults, and any genuine blockers. A blocker is a missing decision that prevents safe work. At the cap, stop the interview and show those blockers together; do not hide them or keep opening more rounds.

Cutting one short mid-run

Sometimes the long interview arrives anyway, because the agent decided to ask first and cap never. You do not have to sit through it. Send the cut, in the same numbering:

Stop asking new rounds. Summarize the answers so far. For
questions 7 onward, propose low-risk defaults under "Decided by
you" (the agent), and list risky unresolved choices as blockers.
Show the spec now and wait for my approval. Do not build yet.

Then actually read the "Decided by you" list — that is the whole point. Overriding one line there costs seconds; discovering the wrong default after the build costs the build. Steering mid-run is lesson 1.10, and "yes to all" with amendments is lesson 1.5.

A real pattern, anonymized

A user asked for an easier-to-navigate, better-looking app. The interview grew to roughly two dozen questions over several rounds. Many recommendations were accepted in batches, but the user overrode three important choices about the opening view, the main activity, and which screen size to prioritize. There was a long pause before the final answers, then implementation began. The record does not prove why the user paused, or that a shorter interview would have produced the same result. It does show where the attention mattered: product direction, not repeated confirmation of defaults. A cap would have forced the remaining questions into a reviewable summary; that is our proposed improvement, not an observed second session.

Recall before you read on

Without scrolling up: name two signs that an interview has run too long, and state the one test you apply to decide whether a question deserves your answer.

Signs include questions answerable from the folder, accepting without reading, or details outside this build. Ask whether the answer materially changes the current build or creates a risk you must own. Delegate low-risk defaults, keep blockers visible, and stop at the agreed cap.

Cap one yourself

Do it now: run a capped interview

You should see the agreed question count, followed by a spec separating your answers, the agent's proposed defaults, and unresolved blockers. Read all three before approving a build. If the agent keeps asking, repeat the boundary and request only the summary. If it starts editing, use the steering procedure from lesson 1.10 and restate that this is an interview only.

The cap is a plain prompt line; no command changes. If questions arrive as a multiple-choice dialog, that is Claude Code's built-in AskUserQuestion tool, and per its documentation questions stay open until you answer them — so the dialog itself is where an uncapped interview stalls.[3] Approval prompts from lesson 0.6 are unaffected.

Same plain prompt. OpenAI's best-practices page recommends ask Codex to question you first and notes that Plan mode lets Codex gather context, ask clarifying questions, and build a stronger plan before implementation — the cap line works the same inside Plan mode.[2] If your version shows questions as selectable options, the numbered reply still works.

omp ships a shortcut for interviews: its documentation says /guided-goal Starts an interview in chat, then configures goal mode from the result.[4] That also starts a persistent goal, so for a capped interview prefer the plain prompt above. Single-vendor source.

The habit

Choose a question cap you will actually read. When it is reached, switch from questions to a short summary: decisions, proposed defaults, blockers. A screenful is a useful personal warning, not a rule; a short question about deleting data can matter more than a page of styling choices.

Check yourself

Question 1 of 3

The agent has asked fourteen questions, and you notice you are accepting without reading. What is the real risk?

A run of unread accepts means the dangerous defaults slip through unnoticed. The interview was meant to surface them; a rubber stamp does not.

Question 2 of 3

For this build you delegated cosmetic defaults and excluded future features. Which question most needs your answer?

Deletion needs your decision because it can destroy data. Under the stated scope, cosmetic choices are delegated and phone support belongs to a later discussion. With a different goal, font size or phone support could be important questions too.

Question 3 of 3

Mid-interview, the list is longer than you will read. What do you send?

The cut keeps the decisions visible: the agent picks defaults, lists them under "Decided by you", and you override in one line — instead of skimming twenty-six questions.

Ask your teacher

Unsure whether a question matters? Paste it and ask: "Would the two answers change any file? If not, take your recommendation and list it as decided by you." Then check the spec — that is cheaper than guessing.
