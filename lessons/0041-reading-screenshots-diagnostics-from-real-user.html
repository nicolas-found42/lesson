Reading a real user's screenshot

A screenshot is a photograph of a symptom. It is not a description of the cause, and it never contains what happened one second earlier. Reading one well is what turns "it's broken" into something you can fix.

About 15 minutesBuilds on: 2.13 Be a first-time userWin: you can turn a vague report into a reproducible one

Why this matters for shipping your app

The last lesson put you in a stranger's seat: you walked your own app as if you had never seen it. Soon the real strangers arrive. One of them sends a picture of a screen and four words, and everything you learn about your app for the next hour comes from that picture. If you read it as a verdict you will fix the wrong thing; if you read it as a clue with gaps, you will ask for the facts that were always missing.

This lesson is about the second reading. It is the moment where a real user becomes a source of evidence, and where most beginners mistake a photograph for a diagnosis.

What a screenshot proves

A screenshot is strong evidence of exactly one kind: appearance. It shows what was on that screen, at that size, at that moment, on that machine. That is not nothing. It rules out a whole family of explanations, including "you are imagining it" and "the text was never there".

It is also, by construction, silent about everything else. Look at what the image cannot contain:

Hidden by the cropWhat it would have told you
What the user did immediately beforeThe sequence that led to the screen, and where it started to go wrong
Which account and which browserWhether the problem follows the person or the app
Requests that failed quietlyWhether something the page needed never arrived
Whether it happened beforeConstant fault, occasional fault, or a one-off

Browsers record part of this for you. Every modern browser ships developer tools that show which assets the page has requested and how long they took to load.[1] One of those panels, the Console, reports the errors encountered as the browser tries to execute your code.[1] The Network panel is the other half: it records every request the page made, so a request that failed appears as a row rather than as a silent absence.[2] None of that fits in a screenshot of the page itself, which is why the picture is a starting point and not the whole report.

The four facts every report is missing

A useful report answers four questions. They are the same four in every tool, because they describe the fault rather than the software.

  1. Steps, in order, from a fresh load. Not "I clicked around", but the exact clicks and the page each one landed on.
  2. Expected against seen. "I expected the list to show my three saved items; I saw an empty box."
  3. When, and how often. Their local clock time, their time zone, and whether repeating the steps reproduces it. A timestamp without a zone cannot be matched against a log line from another machine.
  4. Where. Device, browser, whether they were signed in, and whether a second browser shows the same thing.

People do not send these because nobody asked. They send the picture because it is what upset them. Ask for the rest without making them feel interrogated, and ask once, in one message.

A real pattern, anonymized

A real pattern, anonymized

A small internal app passed every automated check its author had written. Then a teammate tried to connect the app to an outside service by pressing a button, and nothing happened. She sent two screenshots and a small diagnostics file the app could export: the button, the empty panel after pressing it, and a text file of timestamps. Her message arrived mid-afternoon, roughly an hour and a half before anyone acted on it.

The screenshots showed the symptom precisely, and none of it explained the cause. The missing piece was a request the page made to the app's server, which the server refused to answer for the browser. Every test had spoken to the server directly and passed; only a real browser, with a real signed-in account, made the request in the way that failed. The clue that pointed at it was not in the picture at all. It was in the gap between "the button does nothing" and a network panel that would have shown a request leaving and no answer coming back.

The report was usable anyway. It had a who, a what, and a when, plus a file the app itself produced. That is better than most. The remaining work was to spot the question it did not answer, and answer that one before touching code.

Recall before you read on

Cover the table above. Name two things a screenshot of your app's page proves, and two things it cannot tell you. Then say which one of the four missing facts you would ask for first, and why.

It proves appearance — this text or this blank box was on that screen, at that window size, at that moment — and it proves the user was there. It cannot show the steps that led to it, the account or browser, failed requests, or whether the fault repeats. Ask for the steps first: without them you cannot reproduce anything, and reproduction is what separates a fix from a guess.

Give the agent the report, not your theory

When you hand this to your agent, the temptation is to compress: "the connect button is broken, probably the login". That sentence throws away the only good material you have. Paste the report as it arrived — the words, the screenshot, the diagnostics file — and state what you do not know.

Here is a report from a real user, pasted verbatim, with their screenshot attached.

1. Do not change any code yet. First restate the report as: steps, expected, seen, when, where.
2. List what the report does not tell us, and which of those missing facts could change the diagnosis.
3. Tell me the smallest thing I can ask the user that would settle the biggest unknown.
4. Then try to reproduce it yourself in a browser, and tell me what you saw.

Ask me for anything you need. Do not fill a gap with a guess.

Step 1 is the discipline. Rewriting the report in a fixed shape exposes the holes immediately: usually there is no "expected", and the "steps" are a guess at what the user did. Step 4 matters because an agent that reproduces the fault in a real browser has evidence, while an agent that reads a screenshot has only the same picture you do.

Attaching the picture differs slightly across the three agents, so learn where the button is before a user is waiting.

You can paste a screenshot straight into the prompt, or drag the image file into the terminal. Anthropic's guidance lists An image: paste the screenshot of the design or the error into the prompt.[3] Say what the picture is for; a screenshot with no sentence around it is context without a question.

Codex takes a picture with the first prompt through a flag, and later ones by pasting into the composer: Pass an error screenshot, architecture diagram, or design reference with the first prompt, or paste an image into the interactive composer.[4] Attach the user's screenshot, not a description of it.

Paste the image with Ctrl+V, or drag it in, and a chip in the composer confirms the attachment. You can also mention the file by path, which omp reads with the turn.[5] Its documentation notes that Image input is normalized and resized by default, so small text may become unreadable; type out any error message that matters.[5]

Do not fix the first theory

A report that arrives with a symptom invites a story. The story feels like progress and costs a day. In one long session, a user reported that a sign-in kept expiring after roughly fifteen hours. The agent produced three plausible causes and a remedy that deleted files. One short test of the failing call showed that none of the three was it: a company-wide policy forced a fresh sign-in on a schedule. The wrong answer had been confident, specific, and two turns long.

The habit that prevents this is cheap. Before editing anything, write down the one test that would separate your theory from its alternatives, and run it. If the report says "sometimes", the test has to run more than once. Anthropic's workflow page gives the same three pieces of input for a bug: the command to reproduce the issue, steps to reproduce the error, and whether it is intermittent or consistent.[6] Those are exactly the facts a screenshot cannot carry.

One sentence to keep

A screenshot tells you what the user saw. Only a reproduction tells you why.

When the report is thin and the user is gone, do not guess. Send one precise question and do something else meanwhile. Lesson 9.8 turns this into a standing triage prompt; lesson 2.11 covers the case where your own checks were green and the user was still right.

The user's screenshot is their private life

A phone screenshot of your app usually contains more than your app. There may be a name in the corner, an email address, another tab with a document open, a notification from a colleague. Treat the picture as belonging to the person who sent it. Crop to the part that matters. Do not paste it into a public issue or a shared chat without asking. When you need to show it to your agent, that is fine — the agent works in your project — but say plainly that the file is private, and do not commit it into the repository.

This is lesson 2.8's rule from the other side. There, the screenshot was evidence the agent produced for you. Here it is evidence a person produced, and it carries their details along with the fault.

Do it in your own agent

In most browsers the tools open with F12, Ctrl+Shift+I, or on a Mac ⌘+⌥+I; the Console is a tab inside them.[1] The second answer should cite a specific failed request or error line, and the first should contain a list of questions rather than a diagnosis. If the first answer jumps to a fix, that is the failure this lesson is about.

Check yourself

Question 1 of 3

A first user sends a screenshot of an error and says "it's broken". What does the picture prove by itself?

A screenshot proves appearance at one moment: this text was on that screen. Everything about the cause, the account, the sequence and the repetition is outside the frame.

Question 2 of 3

You cannot reproduce the fault from the report. Which request adds the most?

Steps turn a symptom into something you can repeat. Duration and taste do not change the diagnosis, and a video is a nicer version of the same missing information.

Question 3 of 3

Your agent proposes a fix after reading one screenshot. What is the safer first step?

A theory without a reproduction is a guess with good manners. Reproducing first is what turns the report into a test that can fail, and a test that can fail is what a fix needs.
