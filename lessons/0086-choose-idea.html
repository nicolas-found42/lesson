Unit 7 · Lesson 7.1 · First-ship path ★

Choose your capstone idea

Everything you have learned so far was practice. Now you pick the app you will actually take from a blank folder to a public link. The skill in this lesson is choosing an idea small enough to ship — and knowing the fallback if you cannot choose.

About 15 minutesBuilds on: M.1 Ship a hello world page and the other ★ lessons, especially 1.1 Goal + audience, 4.1 Definition of done, 4.2 Scope creepWin: you can name your capstone idea, its audience, and its "done when" line — or take the fallback

Why the choice comes first

The course mission is to take one idea to a public app by directing a coding agent. Units 0 through 6 taught you the moves: prompt with a goal and audience, verify with your own eyes, save your work with git, and ship a static site to a live URL. The capstone (lessons 7.2 and 7.3) is where you run all of those moves on your own project.

That makes this the highest-leverage decision you will make in the course — and also the easiest place to get stuck. Beginners often stall before the first prompt, waiting for the perfect idea. OpenAI's Codex guidance opens its own prompting advice with the question, Goal: What are you trying to change or build?[1] You need an answer to that before anything else. The good news: for learning purposes, the choice matters far less than finishing. A finished modest app teaches more than an abandoned ambitious one.

What makes an idea shippable for you

You are looking for an idea that passes four tests. If a candidate fails one, shrink it (lesson 4.2's scope ladder) or pick another.

Recall before you read on

From lesson 6.1: what does "static" mean for a web app, and why does this course ship static apps first? Answer in your own words before revealing.

A static app is a fixed set of files (HTML, CSS, JavaScript) served the same way to every visitor, with no server-side program or database of its own. It is shippable first because hosting it is free and simple — GitHub Pages serves files from your repository[4] — and because it removes whole categories of failures (secrets, servers, databases) you have not yet learned to handle.

The four tests, in order:

1. It works as a static app. Everything you have shipped so far lives on GitHub Pages, which serves files from a repository.[4] Your capstone should not need accounts, a private database, or a paid service. A quiz, a recipe box, a habit tracker that saves to the browser's own storage, a schedule for one club — all static. Anything needing logins or shared data across people is not, yet; Unit 8 covers why.

2. It has one audience you can name. Lesson 1.1 taught "goal + audience, not method". "So my club members can check rehearsal times on their phones" is an audience. "A social platform" is not. You will reuse this sentence in your first capstone prompt.

3. You can write its "done when" in one line. Lesson 4.1: acceptance criteria before building. If you cannot write a single sentence like "done when I can add a plant, see it in the list, and mark it watered", the idea is still two ideas.

4. You would use it, or know someone who would. This is your verification fuel. In lesson 2.13 you learned to walk the app as a first-time user; that is much easier when you actually know what correct behaviour looks like.

Two ideas, one passes

Idea A: "A travel social network." Needs logins, a shared database, photo uploads, and moderation. Fails tests 1 and 3. You would spend the capstone fighting problems Unit 8 has not taught you to solve yet, and never reach a live link.

Idea B: "A one-page packing-list builder for my own trips, saved in this browser." Add items, tick them off, keep a few saved lists. Passes all four tests: static, one named user (you), one-line "done when", and you know exactly what right looks like.

Shrink before you commit

Most stuck learners are not out of ideas; they are out of small ideas. Apply the scope ladder from lesson 4.2: if your idea is a product, ask what the plugin-sized version is; if that is still big, what the demo-sized version is. Then apply lesson 4.3 and say "and stop there" out loud. Anthropic's agent-building guidance stresses building with guardrails and testing in a bounded environment[5] — a tiny, well-specified project is exactly that kind of boundary for you as a director.

Claude Code's best-practices page describes the classic beginner failure — the agent reported done, but it had no way to check its own work.[3] A crisp one-line "done when" is what gives it (and you) that way.

The fallback example

If you cannot choose in the next ten minutes, take the fallback. It exists so that no learner reaches lesson 7.2 without a project. It is deliberately plain, and it still exercises every skill on the first-ship path.

The fallback: a single-page recipe card

One page, one recipe. It shows the ingredients as a checklist a cook can tick on a phone, the steps in order, and a servings control that rescales the ingredient amounts. Audience: you, cooking with messy hands and a phone on the counter. Done when: ticking items persists after reloading the page, and changing servings from two to four doubles every listed amount. Everything is static; nothing leaves the browser. The final recipe can be your own, so the content is yours even if the template is not.

The fallback's value is honesty about scope: it is the smallest thing that still needs an interview, a plan, real verification, and a deploy. If your own idea is bigger than the fallback, shrink it until it is only modestly bigger — never until it is smaller.

Commit in writing

Do not hold the choice in your head. Write a three-line project card — idea, audience, done-when — and keep it in the project folder. Lesson 1.12's rules file is the natural home: the agent reads it every session, so your goal travels with the repo.

Do it now: choose and write the card

In your project folder (a fresh folder is fine; lesson 0.3), start your agent and paste this prompt:

I want to plan my first real app. Do not build anything yet.
Interview me with numbered questions, each with a recommended
answer, to pin down: the one-line idea, who it is for, the
single sentence "done when", and what is explicitly OUT of
scope. Cap the interview at six questions. When we agree,
write the answers as a short card at the top of AGENTS.md
(or CLAUDE.md if I use Claude Code), and stop there.

What you should see: six or fewer numbered questions with recommendations, then a short card written into your rules file and a stop — no files built, no git activity. If it starts building, interrupt (lesson 1.10) and repeat "do not build anything yet".

Keep approval prompts on throughout; a planning session should not need any file edits beyond the card itself.

You are the decider

The agent can recommend features, structure, and libraries. It cannot know which idea you care about. Lesson 1.6: recommendations are inputs; product intent is yours. If the interview's recommended answers describe a different app than you pictured, override them.

Check yourself

Question 1 of 3

A friend suggests a capstone idea that needs user accounts and a shared database. By this course's rules, what is the issue?

The problem is deployment shape, not difficulty: static hosting serves fixed files to everyone, and accounts need a server plus secrets — Unit 8 territory. Shrink to a static version or pick another idea.

Question 2 of 3

Your idea is "a fitness app for everyone with sharing and challenges". Which fix follows the scope ladder?

The ladder moves the idea down a level (product → personal tool) so it passes the four tests. Budgets and reviewers are good habits from Units 4 and 2, but neither fixes an oversized idea.

Question 3 of 3

Ten minutes in, you still cannot pick between three ideas. What does this lesson say to do?

The fallback exists precisely so indecision never blocks lesson 7.2. Combining ideas recreates the scope problem, and the choice is a product decision the agent cannot make for you.

Ask your teacher

Try: "Here are my three candidate ideas — which is smallest to ship as a static app, and what would you cut from each?" or "Turn my idea into a one-line 'done when'." Advice is fine; the decision stays yours.
